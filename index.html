    1.Context

    The Problem

    Injured people call law firms at night and hit voicemail. The ones who do sign up miss doctor’s visits, which weakens their case.

    What Gideon Does

    An AI receptionist that answers every call, keeps each client’s treatment on track, and holds the whole case in one CRM.

    Where I Come In

    The product was completely vibe-coded when I joined, as the Founding Product Designer. I was responsible for restructuring the app, redesigning its busiest screens and building the design system, and I built those changes in code with Claude Code and merged them into the app myself.

    What I Knew, and What I Didn’t

    What I Was Working From

    I sat in on the calls our CEO had with law firms, and listened back to Gideon’s own recorded calls, so I knew how a case sounds before it reaches a screen.

    What I Was Missing

    No coordinator was using Gideon yet, so I never watched a real one work. Marcus and Daniela came out of those calls, not out of interviews.

    How I Covered the Gap

    I designed from the calls, checked every crowded screen against both personas, and shipped in small pieces so our first firm could correct me.

    2.User Personas

    The owner who pays for Gideon and the coordinator who uses it every day need different things. When a screen got crowded, I checked it against both.

    3.User Flows

    On Saturday morning, Daniela picks up Ryan’s case. The flows show exactly how it moves, with each decision that changes what happens next, so every screen I designed has one job and every hand-off has an owner. Press play to walk Ryan through it.

    4.The Flow, End to End

    Gideon · Appointments

    5.Competitor Study

    These firms already run their cases on a system like this one, so I opened a case in it and worked through the day a coordinator has. I was looking for what makes that day slow.

    So I gave Gideon’s screens one job: say what needs doing, who does it next, and what Gideon has already done. If something on the screen did none of that, I cut it.

    6.Problem Solving

    I started with the 3 interfaces case coordinators use and open most, so the first fixes would help the most. Across all 3, I held to one rule from Daniela’s quote:

    The AI does the work, and a person stays in charge.

    Every call Gideon makes can be approved before it runs, seen afterwards in View Calls, and taken over when it fails.

    01Problem One
    Problem One

    Creating Appointments

    1. Ryan calls
    2. Ryan signs
    3. Visit bookedThis problem
    4. Calls confirmed
    5. Lawyer checks

    In Ryan’s first week, Daniela needs to book his physio visits and set a confirmation and a follow-up call for each.

    Creating an appointment asked for a handful of details, editing it showed every field at once, and neither form could log a past visit or schedule its calls. I rebuilt both as one modal with a Create and an Edit mode, and rewrote the statuses so a coordinator can tell at a glance whether a visit is still coming, already happened, or needs chasing.

    02Problem Two
    Problem Two

    Managing Appointments and Tasks

    1. Ryan calls
    2. Ryan signs
    3. Visit booked
    4. Calls confirmedThis problem
    5. Lawyer checks

    During Ryan’s treatment, Gideon’s calls to him wait for Daniela’s approval, next to her other cases.

    Neither list said where its work stood or what to do next, and Appointments sat under Tasks even though coordinators open them first and most often. I gave Appointments a tab of its own in Treatment, and made every item in both lists show its state with the next action beside it.

    Problem Three

    Simplifying the Client Profile

    5. Lawyer checksThis problem

    The Client Profile is where coordinators spend their day: every list, appointment and call opens into it, and it holds everything the firm knows about a client. Getting one case from the first call to a cheque ran through six of these screens, and each one carried sections nobody opened. I audited the lot and rebuilt it as three stages.

    The app couldn’t pause for a rebuild, so I moved each screen onto the tokens as I redesigned it.

    8.Impact

    • Design system settokens, global and local components, on every screen I redesigned
    • Design foundations setthe information architecture, the user journey and the UX patterns the app follows
    • Major product flows simplifiedfirst call to signed client, booking a visit, and the client profile
    • The 3 screens coordinators live inrebuilt, and merged into the live app in code
    • One status modelone status that says where a visit stands and who acts next

    9.Reflection

    The redesign never met a real caseload, and 3 things would have to hold up first.

    • Fewer statuses, less reason

      A visit stalled by a quiet clinic now reads the same as one stalled by a quiet client. The reason only lives in the call notes, and it needs a line under Escalated.

    • Moving on with tasks open

      A case can reach Settlements with records still missing, and nothing warns anyone. It needs a check on the stage move that lists what is open, without blocking it.

    • A calendar I couldn’t test

      I only ever saw Month, Week and Day with a handful of visits. On a real firm’s week they will be too dense to read, and I never designed that state.

    What I Learned Pushing 100 PRs in a Single Day as a Designer

    September 20265 min read

    At Gideon, an AI receptionist for law firms that represent people hurt in accidents, we had to onboard a client at 2 AM the next morning, and we started working at 11 AM that day. So we had roughly 15 hours to get the product into a state where we could confidently put it in front of a real client. There was just one problem: the app had a lot of functional, design, and foundational issues because most of it had essentially been vibe coded.

    So that morning, the CTO, an engineer, and I sat down and made a pretty simple decision: we were going to fix as many of the important problems as we possibly could that day.

    First, We Cleared Out the Noise

    We had around 10 features in the app, but the client only needed five. We could have left all 10 there and dealt with questions later. But then the client would inevitably ask, “What does this do?” or “Why is this here?” and we’d either have to explain features they didn’t need or be answerable for parts of the product we hadn’t had time to properly fix.

    So we decided to remove the noise first. We hid everything the client didn’t need from the front end and focused only on the five features that were actually relevant to their workflow.

    This is where the information architecture and user journey map I had created for the product became much more valuable than I initially expected. They gave us a framework for making decisions under pressure. The product had features that existed, but the IA and journey map helped us understand which of those features actually belonged in the experience and which ones didn’t need to be there at all. We used that framework to keep the five essential features and hide everything else. Five features meant five things we actually had to worry about that day.

    Then We Started Going Feature by Feature

    Once we had reduced the surface area, we started going through each feature and flow one by one. I would take a particular section of the product, run a UX audit and product test, find the problems, and then prompt an AI agent to make those changes directly on the front end.

    And this is where I learned something that I hadn’t fully understood before: when AI can build almost anything you ask for, the hard part isn’t building anymore. It’s knowing what to ask for.

    The first part of that was understanding the problem before asking AI to solve it. During every UX audit, I had to understand the problems within that particular flow, screen, or section. What does the user actually want to do? What don’t they need? Where could they get confused?

    That meant I couldn’t just look at a screen and say, “This doesn’t feel right.” I had to understand why it didn’t feel right and what the user was actually trying to accomplish. Only after I had that clarity could I decide what needed to change.

    Then Came the Interesting Part: Explaining It to the AI

    Once I knew what was wrong, I had to clearly articulate the solution to the agent that was actually going to make the change on the product. And there were two broad kinds of changes I found myself making.

    The first was editing something that already existed. Maybe it was a UI layout problem, a UX copy issue, a design bug, a confusing interaction, or something else that could be fixed within the existing structure. Those were relatively straightforward because the system already existed. I could point to what was wrong, explain what needed to change, and let the agent make the modification.

    The second was adding or removing something altogether. Removing something was still relatively easy. But adding something new was where you really had to know what you were doing, because the AI had to make a lot of decisions if you didn’t make them yourself.

    Take something as simple as adding a new text field to a form. Let’s say there are already three fields sitting in a single row. Where does the new field go? Does it sit beside the existing three? Does it move below them? Do we turn the layout into a 2×2 grid? Should the new field have the same width? Should the other fields move? Does this change the hierarchy of the form?

    “Add a new text field” sounds like a perfectly clear instruction. It isn’t.

    If I don’t make those decisions, the AI will make them for me. And that’s the important distinction. The AI might produce something that is technically correct and looks perfectly reasonable, but still be wrong for the product.

    So I had to get much better at articulating exactly what I wanted. Not just what needed to change, but how it needed to change, where it needed to go, why it needed to be there, and what it should or shouldn’t affect around it.

    And Then I Had to Review Everything It Changed

    The final part of the process was reviewing what the AI actually gave me. This sounds obvious, but it became one of the most important parts of the workflow.

    Every change exists inside a larger product. If I ask the agent to change one form, that change shouldn’t accidentally affect another form. If I change a component, I need to know where else that component is being used. If I introduce a new interaction, I need to make sure it doesn’t conflict with an existing flow.

    So the process wasn’t simply “find problem → prompt AI → done.” It was much closer to: understand the problem → decide on the solution → articulate it clearly → let AI execute → review what it changed → make further changes without breaking everything around it.

    And that, more than the 100 PRs, was probably the biggest thing I learned from that day.

    When execution becomes extremely fast, the value shifts to the decisions that happen before and after it. You need to understand the problem, know why you’re solving it, be able to communicate the solution clearly, and have enough product understanding to recognize when the AI got it wrong.

    And by 2 AM, it all worked. Every feature and every structure the client needed for the demo was ready, and we got there in time.

    Noir Film Festival

    RoleVisual identity, art direction TimelineMay – June 2026 TypeSelf-initiated

    About the Festival

    Film noir is less a genre and more a mood. It is a term used to describe a genre of films that emerged in the United States in the 1940s and 1950s, which refers to the dark and moody themes and visual style of these films. The Noir Film Festival creates a space to experience cinema at its most atmospheric and introspective, where style and storytelling blend into something unforgettable. It brings together classic and modern films that explore shadow, morality, and human complexity, allowing audiences to see how these themes have evolved over time.

    Problem Statement

    We need a noir film festival because classic noir and neo-noir stories are becoming culturally fragmented and visually underappreciated in mainstream cinema spaces. As a designer, I see an opportunity to create a curated experience that preserves the genre’s cinematic language while introducing contemporary audiences to its influence on modern film, fashion, music, and visual culture. The problem is not the lack of films, but the lack of immersive platforms that make noir accessible, relevant, and emotionally engaging today.

    Scope of Work

    The scope of this project focuses on creating a cohesive visual identity and experience for a noir film festival, from concept to execution. It includes developing the festival’s branding, typography, colour palette, and key graphics, along with applications across posters, tickets, social media, and environmental design. The aim is to capture the essence of noir through every touchpoint, ensuring a consistent, immersive experience that reflects its dark, moody, and cinematic character.

    #ED2024

    #F2ED32

    Black and white form the foundation, referencing the high-contrast cinematography and moral ambiguity that define noir. The deep red represents blood, violence, danger, and the underlying tension that drives many noir narratives. Yellow recalls the glow of streetlights, neon signs, cigarette-lit alleys, and hazy urban nights. Purple carries a more psychological and symbolic layer: seduction, greed, ambition, and the allure of the femme fatale, a recurring figure whose charm often conceals manipulation and betrayal. Together, these colours capture the darkness, glamour, suspense, and emotional complexity that make film noir so distinctive.
