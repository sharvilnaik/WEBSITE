Context

Gideon is an AI receptionist for plaintiff personal-injury law firms. It answers inbound calls, qualifies and signs leads, then works the phones for months chasing providers, medical records and adjusters, writing everything back into the firm’s case management system.

The users are case managers and paralegals carrying 60–120 open files. They’re judged on cases moving, not cases understood, and they’re mostly not in the product. They log in to supervise work that has already happened, which makes the interface a place to decide whether to trust work rather than a place to do it.

Product Designer and Design Generalist

Design Approach

When I joined, the product was one big mess to fix. It was three months old, with vibe-coded layouts and a dozen overlapping sections across the app. The UX copy was mostly AI slop or dev language. The features on offer were yet to be finalised. There was no brand or visual system in place, and no design system to use.

It was the opportunity of a lifetime — to take part in product planning and set up the branding, visual design, UX foundations and UI design of a whole product, autonomously. But doing all of that as a one-person design team is an invitation for more mess. So before designing anything, I set up the structure of my process and workflows, and how I wanted to tackle each problem at hand.

Competitor Analysis

I conducted a thorough competitor research covering the incumbents, the funded challengers, and the answering services buyers were actually comparing us against. Every figure was labelled with its source, because almost nothing in this category is independently reported.

Two findings changed how we thought about the market. Our nearest competitor was behind us on capability but ahead of us on story: they had named a category and listed their integrations in plain language, which is why buyers found them first. The honest limit of the research was that it was desk research alone. No user contact.

I mapped the case manager’s journey end to end, from a lead arriving to a case settling. For each stage, what the AI handles, where the person takes over, and where the experience quietly breaks was clearly defined, based on the feature claims we finalised.

Information Architecture

The app had grown into a complex surface. It had a sidebar with 10+ sections and a dashboard layered over nested modals, tabs inside tabs, and the same object reachable by four different routes. None of that structure was documented.

The underlying problem was that states had been drawn as places. A tree can only show what contains what, but states don’t contain each other — they move between each other. So every state had to be redrawn once for each route that led to it, and roughly a third of the architecture board turned out to be duplicates.

Separating the product tree from the state machines is what made the rest of the work possible.

Problem Solving

A personal-injury case runs on treatment. The client sees a doctor, a chiropractor, an imaging centre — often for months — and every one of those visits has to be booked, confirmed, chased when it slips, and written down. That record is the case: when the appointment was made, what kind it was, when and where it happens, which provider it is with, and what happened afterwards.

All of it has to live somewhere. Gideon is that somewhere — the CRM and the database the firm works out of, which is also what makes the automation possible. Two surfaces do the writing: New Appointment and Edit Appointment. Both were mine to fix, and this is where the work went.

Final Version

  1. Schedule a visit ahead, or record one that already happened — New Appointment and Log a Visit are the same screen with the same fields, not two places to learn.

  2. Send Confirmation Now stages the call against this appointment immediately; it goes out shortly after you save, tagged so you can see it is queued rather than guessing.

  3. Name, phone and email is all a new client needs here. Everything else belongs on the client record, so booking never stalls on a form.

  4. Providers are searched and created inline, so an unfamiliar clinic does not become a reason to stop and go somewhere else.

  5. Search the ones already in use, or add a new one on the spot — no fixed list to petition someone to extend.

  6. Referral detail, script and instructions stay attached to the appointment and to the client, so the next person picking it up has the context.

Adding what the modal did not have: a client, a provider, a treatment type — each without leaving the form.

If New Appointment is where a visit is written down, Edit Appointment is where it is supervised — and an appointment is not one thing but a sequence of states. Before it could be redesigned, that sequence had to be named.

Existing UI

What was wrong with it

  1. Six panels of inputs, most of them empty on every record. The handful that mattered were buried among the ones that never got filled.

  2. Reminders were fixed presets. No way to name a date and time, no way to add an attempt, no way to see what had already gone out.

  3. SMS and email sat in the form as if they worked. Only calls were wired up, so choosing anything else silently did nothing.

  4. Different layout, different fields, different order. You learned the product twice, and still could not tell what was editable or why.

  5. The one thing a supervisor needs to correct — where this appointment actually stands — was the one thing they could not touch.

  6. If a client needed chasing right now, the modal had no way to do it. You saved, left, and went looking for another surface.

1 2 3 4 5 6
Final Version

What it became

  1. When, client, provider, treatment type. Everything that was never filled came out, and what is left reads in one pass.

  2. Each attempt carries a real date and time you can set, and you can add as many as the case needs. What has already gone out is shown back, with its result.

  3. SMS and email came out until they work. The form no longer offers a choice the product cannot keep.

  4. Identical layout and field order, so creating and editing are one thing learned once — and what is editable is obvious because it never moves.

  5. Set where the appointment stands and the rest responds: confirmation disappears once a visit has happened, follow-up appears in its place.

  6. Send Confirmation Now and Get Follow-Up Now stage a call against this appointment immediately, without leaving the record.

Simplifying Creating and Editing Appointments

Two modals for one object meant two designs, two field sets, and edits with no clear home. Rather than align them, I rebuilt both as a single modal with a Create and an Edit mode — identical layout, one data model underneath.

Considered

Keep two modals, align them

  • Least engineering — nothing structural changes.
  • Two things to keep in sync forever; the gap comes back.
  • Still two mental models for one object.
Chosen

One modal, two modes

  • Create and edit are the same screen — learn it once.
  • Every field has one home; edits are findable.
  • More upfront work to unify the data model.
04

UI Design and UX Foundations for Other Sections of the App

Design System

Originally, screens were assembled from five borrowed libraries with raw hex fills and detached text, so the foundations for colour, type, spacing and states had never been set. Since the product was very early stage, I got the opportunity to build the brand’s design foundations from scratch — and the design system was one of the things I loved building.

I built variables for colour, type and layout, a single type ramp, and a small set of real components built as proper variant sets. I built it from the screens I was already producing rather than up front, so nothing in it is speculative. Every component exists because a screen needed it.

How Features Shipped

At Gideon, design and delivery ran as one loop. I made the product decision, wrote the spec, ran it through Claude Code against the existing codebase, my CTO reviewed the PR and merged it. Once merged, I would review the running product and iterate further. This happened on multiple occasions, when we had to fix multiple structural issues with such a small team and time constraints.

Noir Film Festival

RoleVisual identity, art direction TimelineMay – June 2026 TypeSelf-initiated

About the Festival

Film noir is less a genre and more a mood. It is a term used to describe a genre of films that emerged in the United States in the 1940s and 1950s, which refers to the dark and moody themes and visual style of these films. The Noir Film Festival creates a space to experience cinema at its most atmospheric and introspective, where style and storytelling blend into something unforgettable. It brings together classic and modern films that explore shadow, morality, and human complexity, allowing audiences to see how these themes have evolved over time.

Problem Statement

We need a noir film festival because classic noir and neo-noir stories are becoming culturally fragmented and visually underappreciated in mainstream cinema spaces. As a designer, I see an opportunity to create a curated experience that preserves the genre’s cinematic language while introducing contemporary audiences to its influence on modern film, fashion, music, and visual culture. The problem is not the lack of films, but the lack of immersive platforms that make noir accessible, relevant, and emotionally engaging today.

Scope of Work

The scope of this project focuses on creating a cohesive visual identity and experience for a noir film festival, from concept to execution. It includes developing the festival’s branding, typography, colour palette, and key graphics, along with applications across posters, tickets, social media, and environmental design. The aim is to capture the essence of noir through every touchpoint, ensuring a consistent, immersive experience that reflects its dark, moody, and cinematic character.

Black and white form the foundation, referencing the high-contrast cinematography and moral ambiguity that define noir. The deep red represents blood, violence, danger, and the underlying tension that drives many noir narratives. Yellow recalls the glow of streetlights, neon signs, cigarette-lit alleys, and hazy urban nights. Purple carries a more psychological and symbolic layer — seduction, greed, ambition, and the allure of the femme fatale, a recurring figure whose charm often conceals manipulation and betrayal. Together, these colours capture the darkness, glamour, suspense, and emotional complexity that make film noir so distinctive.
